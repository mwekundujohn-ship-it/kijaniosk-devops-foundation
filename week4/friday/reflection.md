# Reflection

## Where two requirements conflicted

The clash showed up between Requirement 2 and Requirement 5, and Challenge D is where it surfaced. Week 3 put the payments service behind a read-only filesystem so it cannot modify anything outside its own log area. But the service still has to read its own configuration when it starts. My first instinct was to leave that configuration alongside everything else in the read-only region. That looked fine on paper until the service could not read it. The fix was to place the configuration where the sandboxed unit could still reach it. Then I had to confirm, by reading the file *as the service account*, that it was genuinely reachable in the sandbox and not just present on disk. The lesson: hardening is not a set-and-forget setting. A control that is right in isolation can quietly break the very thing it is meant to defend. The only reliable way to know is to test from the same vantage point the service has.

## One sentence, translated for Tendo

For Nia I wrote: *"The service sees the system as read-only and can write only to its own log area."* For Tendo I would write: *"`ProtectSystem=strict` mounts the root filesystem read-only inside the unit's namespace, with `ReadWritePaths=/opt/kijaniosk/shared/logs` carved out as the sole writable exception."* The technical sentence is exact and reviewable. It names the directive and the path, so another engineer can reproduce it. But it drops the sense of why anyone outside engineering should care. The plain sentence gives up that precision in exchange for a risk statement a board can act on. Neither is free. Each is complete for one audience only, which is exactly why the brief asks for both.

## The most fragile handoff

The most fragile handoff is the SSH boundary between the control plane and the targets. Specifically, the operator's address being allow-listed on the security group. The pipeline assumes the machine running Ansible can reach the servers on the management port, and that grant is pinned to the operator's public address at plan time. In a real environment that address moves. A VPN, a load-balanced office egress, or a CI runner will present something different, and the first task fails as "unreachable" with no useful error to explain why. What makes it fragile is that it is silent. Nothing in the pipeline notices the allow-list has gone stale, it just cannot connect. To make it safer I would first need to know how operators and any automation actually reach these hosts. A stable bastion or jump host, a VPN or private address space, or an access method built on machine identity. With that answered, I would stop pinning one person's address and route management access through that known, stable entry point. The management port would only be reachable from a bastion subnet, or only from inside the private network.
